Strong structural ancestors
Three non-animal games may be more mechanically relevant than most animal games.
This War of Mine has the closest fundamental rhythm. During the day, named civilians maintain a shelter; at night, one leaves on a dangerous scavenging mission and brings resources and consequences home. It is essentially sanctuary → exposure → return under another theme. MEDIAN makes that rhythm less relentlessly bleak and gives the home a constructive civic future.
RimWorld is the clearest precedent for player attachment to a small group of individually tracked colonists. It models relationships, moods, injuries, chronic conditions, prosthetics, environmental events, and caravans as ingredients in an emergent story generator. MEDIAN’s distinction is restraint: fewer citizens, less combat, clearer dramaturgy, and a deliberate Record rather than stories arising from unrestricted simulation.
Wildermyth is the nearest precedent for citizens becoming histories. Its heroes acquire relationships, permanent transformations, maiming, children, old age, memory, and eventual legendary status. MEDIAN’s Tales, Distinctions, After-names, and memorial archive occupy related territory, but Wildermyth is a tactical heroic RPG rather than a colony game.
TTRPG and physical-game precedents
Here the overlap becomes more direct.
The Warren is the unavoidable TTRPG comparator. It explicitly presents intelligent rabbits confronting predators, seasons, humans, and other rabbits; describes itself as a game of survival and community; draws on Watership Down; and follows changing generations. A MEDIAN TTRPG would need to distinguish itself through the shared colony, highway ecology, scrap material culture, multiple species campaigns, Crossing, Records, and the domestic/expedition cycle.
Bunnies & Burrows is the historical predecessor: intelligent rabbits dealing with natural hazards and larger creatures. It is less threatening as a direct contemporary comparison, but historically important.
Mouse Guard RPG uses patrols and missions through animal territories and offers extensive setting material. Its medieval institutions, weapons, heroic duties, and mission structure are exactly where MEDIAN would diverge.
For cards, Earthborne Rangers is the strongest formal precedent. It is a cooperative customizable campaign card game in which a character’s deck reflects personal history and accumulates equipment, skills, and memories while exploring a living open world. That supports the earlier conclusion that MEDIAN fits a cooperative persistent campaign game better than a competitive rarity-driven TCG.
Aftermath is also relevant: cooperative small-animal expeditions through abandoned human-scale environments affect the state of a home colony. Everdell, Creature Comforts, and Root occupy the woodland-animal tabletop shelf aesthetically, but their underlying purposes—engine building, seasonal worker placement, and asymmetric warfare—are much farther away.
Competitive conclusion
The comparisons most likely to arise are:
Whiskerwood for the screenshot, This War of Mine for the loop, RimWorld for the citizens, Wildermyth for personal history, and Rain World for animal-scale danger.
But none supplies MEDIAN’s complete proposition.
Its most defensible distinguishing features are:
	•	a real and immediately recognizable highway-median ecology;
	•	one permanent, emotionally central colony;
	•	animal civilization without medievalism or industrialization;
	•	traffic functioning as weather and boundary;
	•	low population with every citizen remembered;
	•	action Crossing separated from resolution-based Encounters;
	•	exposure opening both heroic and harmful outcomes;
	•	folklore functioning as tutorial and cultural memory;
	•	a corridor civilization culminating in a plausible animal metropolis.
The largest resemblance risk is currently Whiskerwood. The best defense is not to avoid mice or colony building; it is to foreground what Whiskerwood cannot be mistaken for: wet asphalt, live traffic, three species, intimate population, ecological scrap culture, and sanctuary/exposure/return.

The real ecology is closer than the fiction
This was the most striking search result. MEDIAN’s premise is not merely a fanciful use of unlikely ground; there is a substantial scientific literature describing roadsides in almost exactly the paradoxical language of your world.
Road ecology recognizes that a road can simultaneously be:
	•	a lethal surface;
	•	a barrier dividing populations;
	•	a longitudinal corridor;
	•	an edge habitat;
	•	a refuge in an otherwise intensively managed landscape;
	•	a source of noise, light, salt, pollution, runoff and invasive species.
That full constellation falls within the formal field of road ecology or transportation ecology. UC Davis Road Ecology Center
The species-specific findings are remarkably MEDIAN-like:
	•	Wood mice frequently use road verges and tend to move parallel to the road, while avoiding crossing the paved surface. Verge vegetation partially repairs the connectivity that the road itself destroys. That is essentially MEDIAN’s longitudinal/transverse distinction appearing in field data. Wood-mouse movement study
	•	A separate wood-mouse study found that verges contain important microhabitats and function as movement corridors, although the road still reduces overall connectivity. Connectivity study
	•	Small-mammal abundance can be higher in interstate rights-of-way than in adjacent habitat, particularly for mice, voles and shrews. The same attractive habitat can increase exposure to traffic mortality. USGS study
	•	There is even a European study titled “Motorway verges: Paradise for prey species? A case study with the European rabbit.” Study record
	•	Vegetated medians have been documented as small-mammal foraging habitat, while broader transportation research explicitly notes that they may act as temporary refugia during crossings. Vegetated-median study, median report
	•	Median barriers can also trap or confuse wildlife which has crossed the first carriageway but cannot complete the journey. The median is thus not simply “safe” or “unsafe”; it is contingent sanctuary—which feels extremely consonant with MEDIAN. Median-barrier study
	•	Arboreal species really do receive their own infrastructural crossing solution: artificial canopy bridges have been used for squirrels, opossums and monkeys above roads. Smithsonian’s Reconecta project
Is “highway-riparian” the right term?
It does not appear to be an established scientific phrase. The recognized terms are:
	•	road ecology
	•	road corridor ecology
	•	road-verge habitat
	•	right-of-way habitat
	•	linear habitat or linear corridor
	•	road-effect zone
	•	habitat fragmentation
	•	ecological trap
	•	landscape permeability
	•	permeable highway
	•	crossing ecology
But highway-riparian is an excellent authored concept for the sourcebook. It names something those clinical terms do not: the imaginative experience of treating parallel traffic flows as a river system, with banks, islands, currents, floods, spume, seasonal conditions, deposited material and settlements oriented longitudinally along its course.
The metaphor is not ecologically arbitrary. Highway agencies themselves describe road corridors as altering or blocking animal movement, surface water, subsurface hydrology and other natural flows; they also recognize that animals move parallel to roads through corridor habitat. FHWA road-corridor ecology
I would therefore distinguish:
Road ecology is the real science beneath the setting. Highway-riparian is MEDIAN’s name for the civilization and imaginative geography that might arise inside it.
The deepest difference from all the comparisons is that MEDIAN does not ask, “How can animals cross the road?” It asks:
What kind of culture forms when the road cannot be escaped, defeated or allowed to disappear—when it is the river beside which history itself is lived?
That appears to be MEDIAN’s genuinely original territory. The essential nonfiction companion is Ben Goldfarb’s Crossings, a broad and highly readable account of roads as forces reshaping animal movement, behavior, habitat and evolution. Official book page
